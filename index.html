From Hollywood to Home

Five decades of movies, and how Malaysia started watching itself

In March 2023 an actress born in Ipoh walked onto the stage at the Dolby Theatre and became the first Asian woman to win the Academy Award for Best Actress. Malaysia's own entries for the Academy's international feature award have yet to be nominated.

Three years later came a quieter milestone, at home. Between January and July 2026, Malaysian films drew 7.55 million cinema admissions against Hollywood's 6.94 million. Malaysians were watching more of their own films than of Hollywood's.

This is the story of how the world's cinema changed around us, and how we changed with it.

Part one

Where the movies come from

Start with a simple question: where do the films that reach an audience come from?

1 to 3 countries needed for half of the world's widely seen films: one in the 1990s, three today

The answer here comes from TMDB (The Movie Database), a free online film database whose users rate the films they have seen. Unless a chart says otherwise, a film counts once at least 25 people have rated it there, so the charts follow films that found viewers rather than every film ever shot.

The record runs from 1970 to 2022. In the TMDB dataset used here, films released in 2024 or later have no ratings at all and 2023 films have far fewer than earlier years, so 2022 is the last year that can be compared fairly. The 2020s on this page therefore means 2020 to 2022.

Hollywood's grip was not loosening through the 1970s and 80s. It was tightening. On this page, Hollywood means films produced in the United States. Their share of widely seen films climbed from 37.6% in the 1970s to 55.0% in the 1990s, then fell back to 38.8% in the 2020s. The 64% in the chart's title is higher because it counts all of North America, in its single peak year of 1990; across the whole decade North America made up 58.0%. The 1970s and the 2020s look similarly un-American. What changed is who else is in the picture.

In the 1970s the non-American half of cinema was mostly European, with Italy alone accounting for 12.7% of widely seen films. By the 2010s the picture had changed completely. Italy was down to 3.7% and Hong Kong's share had slipped from 2.4% to 0.8%, while India rose from 0.6% to 4.1%, South Korea from 0.1% to 2.4% and Canada from 1.2% to 5.1%. The same share of the whole, an entirely different world.

Part two

Who makes them together, and where they are filmed

Many of those films belong to more than one country. A film made by two countries counts half for each in the charts above, so it is worth asking who makes films with whom.

80.7% of the co-produced films on the map were made by pairs within Europe and North America

Working across borders is not new. The share of films made by two or more countries has moved between 17% and 28% since the 1970s, with no steady rise. What is striking is who works with whom. Pairs within Europe and North America account for 80.7% of the co-produced films on the map, and pairs involving East, South or Southeast Asia for 10.5%. Malaysia's strongest partnerships are five films with China and five with the United States.

There is another map hiding behind these. A film's production country is a matter of money and paperwork. Where the camera actually points is a different question.

Three place names account for 14.6% of every recorded shoot since 1970: Los Angeles, New York City and "California", recorded without a more exact place. The top ten account for 27.3%. North America and Europe together held about nine in ten recorded shoots in the 1970s and still hold about four in five today. Africa has never held as much as 2% of a decade's recorded shoots. Several of the busiest names on the map, among them Vancouver, Toronto and Atlanta, grew into filming hubs partly thanks to tax incentives.

Malaysia appears eight times in fifty years, most famously in Entrapment (1999), filmed at the Petronas Towers and Bukit Jalil LRT station. The others are Redang Island (Summer Holiday, 2000), Kuching (The Sleeping Dictionary, 2003), Kuala Lumpur (Warkop DKI Reborn: Jangkrik Boss Part 2, 2017), Kuala Lumpur and Langkawi (Assassins, 2020), and Bentong (2021, film untitled in the source). Five of the eight are in and around Kuala Lumpur, where their marks overlap on the map even when zoomed to Asia.

Part three

What the movies cost, and what they are about

Comparing budgets across fifty years is harder than it sounds.

7.7% to 88.4% of each year's 20 biggest films cost $100 million or more in 2025 dollars, the 1970s against the 2010s

TMDB records a budget for only 21% of 1970s films but 38% of 1990s films, and the small films added in later decades drag the typical budget down, which makes budgets look as though they fell. So the next two charts follow a fixed group instead: the twenty highest-grossing films of every single year. A 1975 dot and a 2015 dot describe the same kind of thing. All figures are adjusted for inflation to 2025 US dollars.

Between the 1970s and the 2010s the median budget of a year's twenty biggest films rose from an average of $26 million to $207 million, roughly eightfold. Their median box office rose from $218 million to $882 million, about fourfold. The money going in grew nearly twice as fast as the money coming out, which is why the line slides from the 8× line towards the 4× line as it climbs. The drop at the end is 2020–22, pulled down by the pandemic year.

In the 1970s fewer than one in twelve of the year's biggest films cost $100 million or more in 2025 dollars. By the 2010s, nearly nine in ten did.

Money was not the only thing that changed. So did the kinds of films that reach an audience.

The Western collapsed from 2.8% of 1970s cinema to 0.4% today. The documentary went the other way, from 1.8% to 6.6%. Drama led every decade except the 1980s, when comedy briefly took first place.

Part four

Did any of it make films better?

Films now cost far more to make. Are they any better? Two judges can answer: the millions of people who rate films on TMDB, and the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences.

+0.6 points on the average audience rating, out of ten, for a thousand times the budget

Money and quality do move together, but only just. A film costing a thousand times more than an indie scores only about six-tenths of a point higher out of ten: 5.90 for films under $100,000 against 6.53 for films over $100 million. The relationship holds in every single decade, but it is always weak: a bigger budget buys slightly better ratings, not much better ones.

Across five decades the typical film scores between 6.25 and 6.57 out of ten. Budgets grew eightfold; the audience verdict barely moved.

North America and Europe took 97.8% of Academy Award nominations in the 1970s. By the 2020s that was 90.5%, real movement but slow. East Asia went from 0.7% to 3.0%, and South and Southeast Asia, the region Malaysia belongs to, from 0.1% to 2.1% in the 2010s. Malaysia's own entries, as the first chart showed, are still waiting for a nomination.

Part five

What happened here

At home, two things changed at once: the kind of films Malaysians make, and how many Malaysians go to see them.

2.5× growth in cinema admissions for Malaysian films from 2023 to 2025, from 5.87 million to 14.83 million

Drama has topped every decade of Malaysian film-making. The real movement is horror, which climbed from seventh place in the 1990s to second today, while romance slid from fifth to ninth.

Audiences followed. Local films took 16.8% of Malaysian admissions in 2023 and 36.5% in 2025, with attendance rising from 5.87 million to 14.83 million.

Skim Wajib Tayang, the Compulsory or Mandatory Screening Scheme, is FINAS's scheme requiring cinemas to screen approved Malaysian films, normally for 14 consecutive days. According to the Communications Minister, takings from local films shown under it roughly doubled in a single year, from RM125.28 million in 2024 to RM253.31 million in 2025.

Then, in the first seven months of 2026, local films passed Hollywood outright: 7.55 million admissions to 6.94 million, a lead of about 610,000. Five of them had each taken more than RM10 million, led by Tarung: Unforgiven at RM23.59 million.

Kuala Lumpur has 9.6 mapped cinemas per million residents and Putrajaya 8.2, against 2.0 in Sarawak and 1.6 in Terengganu. Putrajaya's figure rests on a single cinema for its 121,000 residents, but Kuala Lumpur's rests on 20. Measured per person, the cinemas themselves are concentrated in the two federal territories at the centre of the Klang Valley.

In closing

The film you buy a ticket for tonight

  • 55.0%Hollywood's peak share of the world's widely seen films, reached in the 1990s
  • 8×Growth in the median budget of a year's twenty biggest films, 1970s to 2010s
  • 36.5%Malaysian films' share of local admissions in 2025, up from 16.8% in 2023

Five decades ago the cinema that travelled the world was mostly American and European. It became more American, not less, right through the 1990s, and then it broke open. The films that reach us now come from a wider spread of countries than at any time since 1970, they cost enormously more to make, and audiences rate them almost exactly as they always did.

And in 2026, Malaysian films sold more cinema tickets at home than Hollywood's did.

What counts as a film here. A film enters the global charts once it has at least 25 ratings on TMDB, so these show the cinema that still reaches an audience rather than raw production output. A film credited to two countries contributes half to each, so shares total 100% and no film is double-counted. Money is adjusted to 2025 US dollars using the annual average of the US consumer price index (CPI-U). The analysis covers 1970 to 2022: in the TMDB dataset used here, films from 2024 onward have no ratings and 2023 films only a fraction of theirs, so the 2020s means 2020 to 2022 throughout.

Regions. Countries are grouped by the United Nations geographic regions into six: North America, Europe, East Asia, South and Southeast Asia, Latin America, and the rest of the world (Africa, Oceania, the Middle East and Central Asia).

Malaysian figures. Admissions are as reported by Bernama from FINAS and the Communications Minister. Skim Wajib Tayang collections are the box-office takings of local films screened under the scheme, as reported by the minister on 24 August 2026; other official statements give different totals for 2024 and 2025.

Boundaries and names. World outlines follow Natural Earth and Malaysian state outlines follow geoBoundaries. They are drawn as those sources supply them and are not a statement on any territorial claim. Circles and line ends on the world maps sit at a point inside each country's largest outline; 16 small countries and territories with no outline at this scale, among them Hong Kong, Macao and Singapore, are placed at their Wikidata coordinates. Figures are labelled "country or region" so that territories and special administrative regions are not described as states. Films from countries that no longer exist, such as the Soviet Union, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia and East Germany, are attributed to a present-day successor so they can be placed on a map. That affects 0.7% of the films charted here and is a mapping convenience rather than a political judgement. Place names on the filming map are shown as Wikidata supplies them and describe geography, not sovereignty.
